The Electromagnetic Spectrum and Cosmic Energy Distribution

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 1–13, which are based on Reading Passage 1 below.

A Throughout the universe, energy flows across vast reaches of interstellar vacuum in the form of electromagnetic radiation. Unlike sound or oceanic tremors, which depend upon physical matter to propagate, electromagnetic waves are oscillations of electric and magnetic fields that travel freely through completely empty space at the universal speed of light (c ≈ 3.0 × 108 meters per second). Everything we see—from distant constellations to the glowing filament of a lamp—originates from luminous objects that emit electromagnetic waves. Yet human vision can register only an infinitesimal fraction of this cosmic radiation: the narrow band known as visible light.

B The complete continuum of radiant energy is termed the electromagnetic spectrum. It comprises seven primary subdivisions, arranged in a continuous gradient according to wavelength and wave frequency. Wavelength represents the physical distance between two consecutive wave crests, whereas frequency denotes the count of waves that cycle past a fixed reference point every second. Because all electromagnetic waves maintain an identical speed when traversing a vacuum, wavelength and frequency exhibit a strict inverse relationship: as wavelength becomes progressively shorter, wave frequency necessarily increases. Crucially, higher-frequency waves pack far higher quantities of photon energy than their low-frequency counterparts.

C Anchoring the low-energy extreme are radio waves. Having wavelengths exceeding 30 centimeters and extending to thousands of meters, radio waves possess the lowest frequencies in the spectrum. Because their elongated wavelengths bend easily around terrestrial obstructions, they serve as the backbone for television broadcasts, maritime navigation, and amplitude/frequency modulation (AM/FM) radio systems. Immediately adjacent are microwaves, ranging from approximately one millimeter to 30 centimeters. Owing to their ability to penetrate rain and cloud cover, microwaves transmit cellular telephone communications, relay satellite telemetry, and excite water molecules in food within domestic microwave ovens.

D Moving further up the energy scale, infrared waves span from roughly one millimeter down to 700 nanometers (1 nm = 10-9 m). Although invisible to the unaided human eye, infrared radiation is readily detected by human skin as radiant heat. The Sun pours enormous quantities of infrared radiation toward Earth, warming the planetary crust and driving global meteorological cycles. Optical visible light occupies the thin intermediate interval between 700 nanometers (red light, lowest visible frequency) and 400 nanometers (violet light, highest visible frequency). This narrow slice is the sole window that human retinal photoreceptors are biologically configured to process.

E Beyond the violet frontier lies ultraviolet (UV) radiation (wavelengths: 400 nm to 10 nm). Because UV waves carry substantial photon energy, they can rupture chemical bonds, stimulate melanin pigmentation in human epidermis, or induce severe cellular sunburns. Fortunately, the gaseous ozone layer (O3) within Earth’s stratosphere absorbs the vast majority of lethal short-wavelength solar UV radiation before it strikes the ground. Next in frequency are X-rays (10 nm down to 0.01 nm). Possessing enough energy to penetrate low-density organic muscle and skin tissues while being halted by dense calcium-rich bones, X-rays are indispensable tools in orthopedic medicine and airport security scanners.

F At the absolute apex of the spectrum reside gamma rays, characterized by sub-atomic wavelengths shorter than 0.01 nm and astronomical frequencies. Gamma rays are generated by high-energy nuclear events, including radioactive decay inside atomic nuclei, supernova explosions, and pulsar collisions. Their intense ionizing energy renders them lethal to biological tissue; however, when controlled with stereotactic precision by medical physicists, focused gamma-knife radiation beams are utilized to destroy malignant cancer tumors without requiring invasive surgical incisions.

Governing Scientific Law: The Universal Wave Equation
c = f × λ   (or   λ = c / f)
Where c represents the constant vacuum velocity of light (3.0 × 108 m/s), f is wave frequency in Hertz (Hz), and λ (lambda) is wavelength in meters. Because light velocity in a vacuum is invariant, frequency and wavelength maintain an exact inverse proportion.

Attenuation, Photometric Intensity, and Atmospheric Chromatics

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 14–26, which are based on Reading Passage 2 below.

A When light departs its radiant emitter and encounters physical matter, its behavior changes dramatically. While an electromagnetic wave in an unblemished vacuum maintains an invariant velocity of 300,000 km/s (186,000 miles/s), its passage through tangible substances forces an immediate reduction in propagation speed. In water, light slows to roughly 225,000 km/s; within optical crown glass, it diminishes to 200,000 km/s; and when traversing the dense crystalline lattice of a natural diamond, it drops precipitously to a mere 124,000 km/s. This optical retardation is driven by continuous microscopic interactions between incoming electromagnetic photons and the orbital electron clouds of surrounding atomic particles.

B Based on their optical properties, macroscopic materials are grouped into three distinct categories: transparent, translucent, and opaque. Transparent substances—such as polished architectural window glass or pure water—permit virtually all incident light rays to pass directly through without significant deflection, allowing observers on the opposite side to view sharp, undistorted forms. Translucent media, exemplified by frosted bathroom partitions, tinted automotive films, or parchment paper, permit a substantial volume of light to penetrate; however, microscopic irregularities within the material scatter the transmitted rays in disordered paths, rendering background objects blurry and indistinct. Opaque materials, such as solid teak doors or concrete masonry, prohibit any light transmission whatsoever; incoming photons are either absorbed as thermal energy or reflected away from the surface.

C A crucial metric in optical physics is intensity, defined quantitatively as the total quantity of radiant energy passing perpendicularly across one square meter of spatial surface in one second. Crucially, intensity is not purely a property of the light bulb or emitter; it is severely governed by the observer’s distance from that source. Because light waves radiating from an isolated point emitter spread uniformly outward across the surface of an expanding sphere, the flux density drops in precise accordance with the inverse-square law (Intensity ∝ 1 / distance2). If an observer doubles their physical distance from a lamp (2d), the illumination area expands fourfold (4A), reducing measured light intensity to exactly one-fourth (25%). At three times the distance (3d), the intensity plummets to one-ninth (approximately 11.1%). "Brightness," by contrast, is the subjective neuro-perceptual interpretation of this physical intensity by the human brain.

D Beyond spatial dilution, sunlight undergoes profound modifications when entering Earth's gaseous envelope. Terrestrial air contains countless diatomic molecules of nitrogen (N2) and oxygen (O2), alongside airborne aerosols. When incoming white solar rays strike these microscopic gas molecules, Rayleigh scattering takes place. Rayleigh scattering efficiency is inversely proportional to the fourth power of wavelength (1 / λ4), dictating that short wavelengths (violet and blue, approx. 400–450 nm) scatter far more vigorously than long wavelengths (red and orange, approx. 650–700 nm). As blue light is cast in all directions across the atmosphere, the sky radiates its characteristic cerulean hue, while the direct solar disc—stripped of its scattered blue rays—appears warm yellow to terrestrial observers.

E Even more astonishing is the geometric deception caused by atmospheric refraction. As solar rays plunge from the vacuum of outer space into the progressively denser gaseous strata of Earth's atmosphere, the light waves steadily decelerate. Entering the curved atmospheric shell at an oblique angle, this continuous deceleration bends the incoming rays downward toward Earth's center of mass. Consequently, when the Sun has physically dropped below the geometric horizon at dusk, its bent light rays continue to arch around the planet’s curvature into an observer’s eyes. Observers on Earth witness the sunset approximately two to three minutes after the physical body of the Sun has already sunk beneath the astronomical horizon line.

F Inside the human eye, this incoming light undergoes its final, critical transformation. Light rays initially pass through the cornea—a transparent convex structure on the eye's outer surface that performs the majority of optical refraction. The rays then traverse the dark pupillary aperture, whose diameter is adjusted by the pigmented muscular iris to regulate incoming illumination. Behind the pupil, the crystalline lens fine-tunes focal convergence via the dynamic pulling of ciliary muscles, focusing crisp inverted images directly onto the retina. The retina’s photoreceptors—rod cells for dim illumination and cone cells for chromatic definition—absorb the photon energy, translating electromagnetic waves into neural electrochemical impulses transmitted via the optic nerve to the visual cortex of the brain.

Governing Scientific Law: The Photometric Inverse-Square Law
I = P / (4π × d²)   ⟹   (I₁ / I₂) = (d₂ / d₁)²
Where I is photometric intensity in Watts per square meter (W/m²), P is total luminous radiant power emitted by the source in Watts, and d is radial distance in meters from the emitter.

Waveform Dichotomy: Comparative Dynamics of Electromagnetic and Mechanical Energy

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 27–40, which are based on Reading Passage 3 below.

A In the classical taxonomy of wave mechanics, physical phenomena that transfer energy across space are partitioned into two fundamentally distinct ontological classes: electromagnetic waves and mechanical waves. While everyday human experience often conflates seeing a lightning bolt with hearing its thunderous accompaniment, these sensory inputs are governed by opposing physical architectures. Light, as established, is an electromagnetic phenomenon composed of oscillating, self-perpetuating electric and magnetic vector fields. Sound, conversely, is an acoustic mechanical wave requiring material inertia and elastic cohesion to propagate.

B The quintessential divergence between light and sound lies in their absolute dependence upon a material medium. Acoustic waves are longitudinal vibrations: they transport mechanical energy via sequential collisions of adjacent atoms, generating alternating zones of high-density compression and low-density rarefaction. Deprived of physical atoms—such as in the vacuum of space—sound cannot manifest. A catastrophic cosmic supernova, though releasing unfathomable energy, transpires in absolute silence. Electromagnetic light waves, by contrast, carry their own field mediators and achieve their peak velocity in empty space, where no physical particles exist to disrupt their propagation.

C A further profound disparity emerges when evaluating how medium density influences propagation velocity. For mechanical sound waves, tighter molecular packing directly accelerates energy transfer. Because sound propagates through physical particle collisions, an acoustic disturbance travels slowest in gases (343 m/s in ambient air), faster in liquids (1,480 m/s in water), and fastest in densely bonded solids (5,960 m/s in steel). In striking contrast, the velocity of light is inversely influenced by material density: electromagnetic waves achieve their unconstrained maximum speed in a vacuum and suffer substantial deceleration as atomic density increases (slowing to 225,000 km/s in water and 200,000 km/s in glass).

D The disparity in raw velocity between the two wave species is staggering. In terrestrial atmosphere, light outpaces sound by an approximate factor of 875,000. This immense gulf is familiar to anyone observing a localized thunderstorm: the discharge of electrostatic potential generates a blinding lightning flash and an explosive acoustic shockwave at the exact same instant. Because optical photons cover a one-kilometer distance in approximately 3.3 microseconds, the flash registers on the human retina instantaneously. The acoustic thunderclaps, advancing at a pedestrian 343 m/s, require approximately three full seconds to traverse each kilometer, creating the noticeable perceptual time gap between sight and sound.

E The physical mechanisms of sensory detection likewise reflect these deep structural differences. The human ear relies upon a mechanical transduction chain: acoustic pressure differentials cause the tympanic membrane (eardrum) to vibrate, which articulates three delicate middle-ear ossicles that mechanically agitate fluid within the cochlea, exciting microscopic hair cell stereocilia. The human visual system, conversely, operates as an electromagnetic quantum sensor. Retinal rod and cone cells contain photosensitive opsin protein pigments that undergo conformational chemical isomerizations upon the absorption of individual light photons, triggering hyperpolarizing neural cascades without requiring any macroscopic mechanical motion.

F Finally, the mechanics of object illumination distinguish optical perception from acoustic perception. An object is classified as luminous if it actively synthesizes and emits its own electromagnetic waves, such as the Sun, chemical combustion flames, or bioluminescent organisms. The overwhelming majority of terrestrial objects, however, are strictly non-luminous: books, furniture, trees, and the Moon generate zero intrinsic visible radiation. Human beings perceive these inert bodies exclusively because external light rays from luminous sources strike their surfaces and undergo diffuse or specular reflection, directing redirected photons into the viewer’s ocular aperture. In acoustic contexts, by contrast, non-emitting bodies do not reflect environmental sound with sufficient coherence to permit detailed passive spatial mapping without active echolocation pulses.

G Recognizing this fundamental dichotomy between electromagnetic transverse radiation and mechanical longitudinal vibrations is pivotal across applied science. While submarine oceanography must utilize acoustic sonar systems because electromagnetic radio waves suffer severe absorption in conductive seawater, deep-space astronomical probes communicate with Earth exclusively via microwave and radio frequencies, navigating millions of kilometers of silent vacuum where sound waves cannot exist.

Propagation in Vacuum Freely propagates (3.0 × 108 m/s) Impossible (requires material particles)
Velocity in Air (20°C) Approx. 300,000,000 m/s Approx. 343 m/s
Effect of Medium Density Decelerates as density increases Accelerates as density increases
Human Sensory Transducer Retinal rods and cones (chemical) Cochlear hair cells (mechanical)
Environmental Visibility Requires emission or surface reflection Requires active emission or sonar echo
Governing Scientific Law: Acoustic-Optical Velocity Differential & Distance Calculation
Distance to Storm (meters) ≈ 343 × Elapsed Seconds   (or   Distance (km) ≈ Seconds / 3)
Because light velocity is approximately 875,000 times greater than sound velocity, the optical transit time across terrestrial distances is practically instantaneous (approx. 0 seconds). Observers can accurately estimate storm distance in kilometers by dividing the seconds counted between lightning flash and thunder by 3.

📖 Bilingual Vocabulary Bank

Electromagnetic Spectrum (สเปกตรัมคลื่นแม่เหล็กไฟฟ้า)
The entire continuous range of electromagnetic waves arranged by frequency and wavelength.
Luminous Object (วัตถุที่มีแสงในตัวเอง)
An entity that actively produces and radiates its own light (e.g., Sun, flashlight).
Non-luminous Object (วัตถุที่ไม่ส่องสว่างในตัวเอง)
An object that does not produce light and is visible only by reflecting light from another source.
Wavelength (λ) (ความยาวคลื่น)
The physical distance between two identical consecutive crests or troughs of a wave.
Frequency (f) (ความถี่)
The number of complete wave cycles that pass a given location per second (measured in Hertz, Hz).
Transparent (โปร่งใส)
Allowing almost all light to transmit cleanly without scattering, forming sharp, clear images.
Translucent (โปร่งแสง)
Allowing light to pass through but scattering rays randomly, causing objects behind to appear blurred.
Opaque (ทึบแสง)
Preventing any light transmission; all incident photons are either reflected or absorbed as heat.
Photometric Intensity (ความเข้มแสง)
The amount of radiant energy delivered per square meter per second; drops with distance squared (1 / d²).
Refraction (การหักเหของแสง)
The bending of a wave's direction when entering a new medium due to an abrupt change in speed.
Transverse Wave (คลื่นตามขวาง)
A wave where particle or field oscillations occur perpendicular to the direction of wave travel (e.g., Light).
Longitudinal Wave (คลื่นตามยาว)
A wave where particle oscillations occur parallel to the direction of energy propagation (e.g., Sound).
Compression & Rarefaction (ส่วนอัดและส่วนขยาย)
Regions of higher particle density (compression) versus lower particle density (rarefaction) in sound waves.
